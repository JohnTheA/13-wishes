# Mapping the evidence for a menopause hormone-therapy "titration companion"

The thesis rests on a chain of load-bearing claims: that a large share of women starting menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) change dose, switch formulation, or quit within the first year; that a meaningful fraction of that churn is preventable by information and monitoring rather than driven by cost, fear, or genuine intolerance; and that a symptom-and-dose logging tool can therefore both improve outcomes and accrue a valuable dose–response dataset. The literature supports the first link only partially and in the wrong currencies (surveys and non-Western cohorts, not Western claims data), gives at best indirect support to the second, and offers a cautionary base rate for the third. Throughout, I flag where a number is a proxy, which country it comes from, and where the Canadian evidence you would want simply does not exist.

## The demand side: is the pain real?

### Pain reality and size

**There is no direct claims- or cohort-based figure for what fraction of Western women change MHT dose or formulation within the first year.** This is the most important gap for your thesis, and the specific number you are building on does not appear in the literature I can locate. What exists is discontinuation data (quitting entirely), mostly from surveys and from non-US/UK/Canada health systems, plus a separate body on *why* women stop that is dominated by self-report rather than dispensing records.

The discontinuation base rates that do exist cluster high but vary by measurement and setting. In a South China cohort, 35.9% of hormone-replacement-therapy users discontinued within one year, a survey-based figure ([Chu 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/13697137.2017.1397622)). The most rigorous first-year number I found is older US prescription-validated survey data: the 12-month discontinuation probability was 62% in women aged 65 and older versus 48% in women aged 50–55 (relative risk 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.6) ([Ettinger 1999](https://doi.org/10.1097/00042192-199906040-00003)). A 2025 South Korean nationwide claims cohort measured persistence directly and found systemic MHT was discontinued after a mean of 13.2 months and local MHT after 2.4 months, with fewer than half of symptomatic women ever prescribed MHT ([Chun 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/13697137.2025.2524165)); this is the closest thing to a real-world persistence signal, but from Korea, not a market you can extrapolate to Canada without a stretch. A 2025 systematic review pooling discontinuation studies reported a 51.3% discontinuation rate over an average 5.4-year duration ([Bunnewell 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/1471-0528.70023)), a multi-year rather than first-year figure. A Swedish register study adds a persistence signal from a Western system, though a narrow one: among women with early menopause who did start hormone therapy, the majority used it for less than one year ([Lindh-Åstrand 2015](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/dev017)). An older US clinical review, meanwhile, framed cessation optimistically, estimating that roughly 75% of women who attempt to discontinue succeed without major difficulty, with rebound vasomotor symptoms preventing it in a subset ([Grady 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2005.09.051)) — a reminder that much discontinuation is intended and symptom-resolved, not a failure an app should try to prevent. The honest synthesis: first-year discontinuation is plausibly in the 30–50% range, but every anchor is either a survey, a non-Western system, or a multi-year window, and none isolates dose changes or formulation switches from outright cessation.

**How much of this is preventable by information versus driven by cost, fear, or intolerance?** The reasons literature is where the thesis is both most supported and most vulnerable, because the reasons that dominate are only partly information-tractable. The 2025 systematic review found the primary discontinuation reasons were healthcare-provider recommendation (31.2%), fear of risks (26.0%), and a preference for a "natural" approach (25.6%) ([Bunnewell 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/1471-0528.70023)). In the South China cohort the leading reasons were fear of breast or uterine cancer (28.4%) and feeling that symptoms had resolved (22.9%) ([Chu 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/13697137.2017.1397622)). A US community-sample prospective study found that over nine months, discontinuation was predicted by low understanding of and confidence in hormone therapy and by the perception that menopause is a natural process ([Bosworth 2005](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10865-005-2721-2)). That last finding is the strongest evidence in your favour, because low understanding is a modifiable, information-shaped driver. But "clinician told me to stop," "I fear cancer," and "menopause is natural" are, respectively, a clinical decision, a risk belief that information campaigns have struggled to move since the Women's Health Initiative, and a values stance. An expectation-setting app can address the knowledge-and-confidence slice; it is unlikely to reach the clinician-driven or values-driven slices, which together outweigh it in every dataset above.

Knowledge deficits themselves are real and large. A 2025 Brazilian survey found only 29% current MHT use, while 92.97% of climacteric women demonstrated little or no knowledge of hormone therapy and only 30.3% were satisfied with the menopause information they had received ([Rodrigues 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0316873)). This establishes the addressable pain (women are under-informed and know it) but it is Brazilian survey data, and the leap from "poorly informed" to "would persist longer with an app" is exactly the leap the outcomes literature below does not yet let you make.

### The prevalence backdrop

The addressable market has been contracting for two decades. US serial survey data show MHT prevalence fell from 26.9% in 1999 to 4.7% in 2020 across all age groups ([Yang 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamahealthforum.2024.3128)). The closest Canadian proxy is a cross-national prescribing analysis: MHT prescriptions declined 67% in Australia and 64% in Manitoba, Canada, from their 2001 peak to 2008, a sustained reduction across all age groups over 50 ([Salagame 2020](https://doi.org/10.1089/jwh.2019.7828)). This is the only Canadian population-level MHT utilization signal I could locate; it is from one province, and it ends in 2008, so it predates the recent cultural resurgence of menopause interest and may understate current initiation. Treat any claim about a large, growing Canadian MHT-initiator population as unverified against Canadian data. That said, real-world use does persist into older age in the US: a Medicare analysis spanning 2007–2020 studied roughly 10 million women using MHT beyond age 65 ([Baik 2024](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002335)), evidence that a long-duration user base exists even as initiation prevalence falls.

## The intervention side: does monitoring work?

### Does monitoring change outcomes?

The best evidence for this second load-bearing claim comes entirely from oncology, not menopause. The landmark result: in a US single-centre randomized trial of electronic patient-reported-outcome (ePRO) symptom monitoring during chemotherapy, health-related quality of life improved in 34% of the intervention group versus 18% of usual care (P<.001), with a mean decline of 1.4 versus 7.1 points at six months ([Basch 2016](https://doi.org/10.1200/JCO.2015.63.0830)), and the follow-up showed an overall-survival benefit ([Basch 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.7156)). A French trial found a survival advantage for web-based symptom monitoring after lung-cancer treatment ([Denis 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.18085)), and the UK eRAPID trial tested weekly online symptom self-reporting with automated advice during chemotherapy ([Absolom 2021](https://doi.org/10.1200/JCO.20.02015)). However, the multicentre US PRO-TECT replication in community oncology was more equivocal, reporting secondary quality-of-life outcomes that did not reproduce the earlier single-centre magnitude ([Basch 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.9265)). The pattern of a large effect in a single motivated centre attenuating on multicentre replication is directly relevant to how you should discount the menopause-app evidence.

The mechanism that drove the oncology benefit was clinician alerting on severe symptoms with intervention, not patient self-education. Your tool's clinician-summary feature aligns with this; its expectation-setting feature does not carry the same evidentiary support. Crucially, none of this is in menopause, where symptoms are not life-threatening and the alert-and-intervene loop is weaker.

Self-titration, closer to your actual mechanism, has a real but narrow evidence base concentrated in hypertension. A US meta-analysis found self-measured blood pressure with support reduced systolic BP by 3.4 to 8.9 mm Hg versus usual care, though self-monitoring alone gave a -3.9 mm Hg effect at six months that was not sustained at twelve ([Uhlig 2013](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-159-3-201308060-00008)), and a Spanish trial showed BP self-monitoring plus medication self-titration reduced systolic BP long-term versus usual care ([Martínez-Ibáñez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.10063)). A 2026 Taiwanese trial of a digital insulin-titration tool found it reduced HbA1c by 0.87% versus 0.43% in usual care (P=0.0109) at 24 weeks, with no difference in insulin adherence ([Lu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pcd.2026.04.012)); the clinical marker moved but adherence did not, a useful caution. And the cost-effectiveness base rate is sobering: the large US REMIND trial found that low-cost reminder devices did not improve medication adherence among 53,480 suboptimally adherent patients ([Choudhry 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.9627)). Passive digital nudges routinely fail; the interventions that work couple monitoring to a clinical action.

### Digital menopause tools

Menopause-specific digital tools show symptom improvement in mostly uncontrolled or short designs. A randomized trial of a mobile digital therapeutic reported that hot-flash severity declined from 16.4 to 13.6 in the app arm versus 19.1 to 17.8 in control over six weeks (treatment effect P=0.01) ([Duffecy 2025](https://doi.org/10.2196/58204)), a genuine RCT but six weeks and symptom-focused. A UK single-arm cohort of a specialist-led app found significant Menopause Rating Scale reductions at 90 and 180 days ([Schei 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.maturitas.2024.108005)), and a longitudinal cohort of the Health & Her app examined engagement-intensity versus symptom improvement in 1,900 women ([Andrews 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2023-077185)). These establish that women engage and self-report symptom relief; none demonstrates that the app changed MHT persistence, dose optimization, or any hard outcome, and the single-arm designs cannot separate the tool from regression to the mean and placebo. A registered US RCT protocol to test whether an app improves menopause knowledge exists ([Karam 2025](https://doi.org/10.2196/76536)), which tells you the outcome question is open, not answered. Across chronic disease, a 2025 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs of medication-adherence apps found the category effective in aggregate ([Lanke 2025](https://doi.org/10.2196/60822)), but adherence apps and MHT persistence are different targets, and the menopause-specific replication has not been done.

## Expansion and bottom line

### The expansion therapies

Your intended expansions have stronger, better-quantified adherence problems than MHT itself, which is worth weighing when you consider sequencing.

For adjuvant endocrine therapy in breast-cancer survivors, non-adherence is severe and well-characterized: a systematic review concluded that nearly half of hormone-receptor-positive patients are non-adherent and over two-thirds discontinue before the recommended five years ([Paranjpe 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10549-018-05073-z)). A US prospective cohort found 18% non-persistence over two years, with higher income protective ([Hershman 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10549-016-3788-x)), and a German real-world study found 13.7% discontinued letrozole early, 73.5% of them due to drug-related toxicity, with 12-month persistence of 86.9% ([Nabieva 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/annonc/mdx630)). Side effects are the documented engine: a review found that estrogen-deprivation side effects (vasomotor, musculoskeletal, genitourinary, fatigue, psychological) persist over time and drive poor adherence and early discontinuation ([Cucciniello 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctrv.2023.102624)). Two Canadian studies exist here, which matters for your evidence-discipline requirement: a Canadian qualitative study captured the ambivalence survivors feel toward the drug ([Toivonen 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/curroncol28040227)), and a Canadian cohort of senior patients found 32% of women aged 70 and over did not persist over five years ([Krotneva 2013](https://doi.org/10.3747/co.21.1605)). This is the one expansion arm with real Canadian discontinuation data.

For GLP-1 receptor agonists, discontinuation is high and cost-sensitive. A 2026 UK cohort found a one-year GLP-1 discontinuation risk of 41.1% overall, higher in people without obesity (49.2%) than with obesity (37–40%) ([Ulrich 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70535)). A US claims study of semaglutide for weight management found 46% discontinued within five months, with rates rising from 41% in the lowest-copayment quintile to 51% in the highest ([Xu 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24315)), a direct demonstration that cost, not information, is a dominant lever here, which cuts against a companion-app thesis for this class. A mixed-methods survey found discontinuation driven by cost, unmet expectations, and side effects ([Naveed 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16405)), and formulation matters: a Japanese cohort found oral semaglutide had significantly higher adherence and lower discontinuation than the once-weekly injection ([Horii 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jdi.14265)).

### Implications and gaps

The evidence supports a qualified version of your thesis and refutes the strongest version. Discontinuation is real and high across all three therapy classes, and low knowledge and confidence are genuine, modifiable contributors in MHT ([Bosworth 2005](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10865-005-2721-2)). Monitoring coupled to clinician action changes hard outcomes in oncology ([Basch 2016](https://doi.org/10.1200/JCO.2015.63.0830); [Basch 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.7156)) and self-titration changes intermediate outcomes in hypertension and diabetes ([Uhlig 2013](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-159-3-201308060-00008); [Lu 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pcd.2026.04.012)). Those are the pillars you can stand on.

But four gaps should temper the pitch. First, the load-bearing number, first-year dose-change or switch rates in Western claims data, does not exist in the literature; every proxy is a survey, a non-Western system, or a multi-year discontinuation figure, and the reasons that dominate discontinuation (clinician advice, cancer fear, "natural" preference) are largely outside an app's reach, with the knowledge-and-confidence slice it can reach being the minority driver. Second, the outcome evidence for digital menopause tools is engagement and short-term symptom self-report, not persistence or dose optimization, and the strongest analogue (oncology ePRO) attenuated on multicentre replication ([Basch 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.9265)) while passive digital nudges failed outright at scale ([Choudhry 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.9627)). Third, in the expansion arm with the cleanest discontinuation data, GLP-1s, the dominant lever is out-of-pocket cost ([Xu 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24315)), which an information tool cannot touch. Fourth, Canadian evidence is nearly absent: one dated Manitoba prescribing series ([Salagame 2020](https://doi.org/10.1089/jwh.2019.7828)) and a small qualitative surgical-menopause study ([Siyam 2018](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000001069)) for MHT, with the only solid Canadian discontinuation numbers coming from the breast-cancer endocrine-therapy literature ([Krotneva 2013](https://doi.org/10.3747/co.21.1605)). Any Canadian market or reimbursement claim in your thesis is currently extrapolated from US, UK, or Korean data.

The most defensible framing is not "we prevent discontinuation" but "we improve the clinician's titration information and capture longitudinal dose–response data." The former overreaches the evidence; the latter is a data-asset play the literature neither confirms nor refutes, which is the appropriate posture for a genuinely novel dataset.
